Two skies, one machine

Left: the Earth as a spinning ball with a distant Sun. Right: the flat model, a still disc with a small Sun circling above it.

Standing in both worlds is the same real machine, an equatorial telescope mount. It can only do one thing: turn one axis at one constant speed. That is enough to track the entire sky, but only in one of these worlds.

The numbered buttons run the demonstrations. Drag to look around, use the slider to change latitude, press space to pause.

What you are looking at

An equatorial mount has one driven axis, the polar axis (brass). Point that axis at the sky's pole and one motor at one constant speed cancels the Earth's rotation, so anything you aim at stays put in the eyepiece. The required tilt of that axis above your horizon is exactly your latitude, everywhere on Earth.

The flat model has to explain the same sky with a still ground. Its telescope needs two axes (ALT up-down, AZ left-right), both speeds changing all the time, and the eyepiece image twists while it tracks. Real numbers for both machines run in the strip below.
